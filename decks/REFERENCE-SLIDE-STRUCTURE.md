# Reference slide structure and style

This document records the structure and wording patterns that the course decks must follow. It is based on a complete visual and structural inspection of the six most recent decks in the presentations archive. The academic decks receive the most weight because the new decks are instructional presentations about linguistic statistics.

## Reference set

1. `chive-atscience2026-talk-march-2026.pptx`, 26 slides
2. `penn-mindcore-talk-jan-2026.pptx`, 290 slides
3. `hltcoe-wip-nov-2025.pptx`, 13 slides
4. `oxford-colloquium-talk-oct-2025.pptx`, 288 slides
5. `naloma-talk-aug-2025.pptx`, 111 slides
6. `mayfest-talk-may-2025.pptx`, 209 slides

The reference decks establish the visual system, pacing, and some useful argument patterns. They do not establish a compulsory structure for course modules. Research presentations and instructional modules do different work. The course decks should transfer the visual discipline of the reference decks while organizing the material around how students encounter and learn each concept.

## Overall organization

Each deck must have a topical title. Decks are not numbered as lectures. A deck that spans more than one class meeting is a module.

A course module normally uses the following order.

1. The title slide gives the topical title, author, affiliation, course, and all meeting dates covered by the module.
2. A `This Module` slide names the meetings or major parts in the order in which students will encounter them.
3. A dark section slide marks each class meeting. It gives the topic and date for that meeting.
4. The meeting begins with a short reminder of the relevant earlier concept or with a linguistic example that creates the need for the new concept.
5. Each new concept is introduced through a sequence of motivation, standard definition, worked example, and interpretation.
6. A brief check asks students to use the concept before the module moves on.
7. The end of each meeting states what students should now be able to do and identifies the question that motivates the next meeting.
8. A `Module Summary` slide closes the complete module. It states the concepts and relations established across the meetings.

Research presentation labels such as `Big Question`, `Challenge`, `Possibility`, `Answer`, and `Upshot` are optional. Use one only when the slide genuinely performs that function and the label helps students follow the argument. Do not impose the full sequence from a research presentation on every topic. In particular, do not use a generic role label in place of a title that names the content of the slide.

## Structure within a substantive part

A substantive part should normally follow this order.

1. A descriptive title names the concept, example, contrast, or operation on the slide.
2. A linguistic example or familiar problem shows why the concept is needed.
3. A definition slide introduces one standard term and its notation. The term must match the terminology used in the notes and statistical literature.
4. A build sequence changes one element at a time. It may add an annotation, reveal one part of an equation, add one point or line to a plot, or contrast the current example with a minimally different one.
5. A worked example carries out the complete procedure on linguistic data.
6. A check asks students to predict the next step, calculate a quantity, or interpret a result.
7. A synthesis slide states the relation among the concepts introduced in the part.
8. A transition identifies the problem that motivates the next part.

This order is cumulative. A definition should not appear before the example that motivates it. A method should not appear before the quantity it estimates has been defined. A result should not appear before the comparison that makes it interpretable.

## Slide roles and wording

The reference decks use a small, stable vocabulary for slide roles. These labels are available when they help organize an argument, but they are not the default titles for instructional slides.

1. `Big Question` identifies the question for the complete deck.
2. `Question` identifies the local question for a part.
3. `Surprising Fact` states an empirical or conceptual observation that creates the problem.
4. `Challenge` states what a current analysis, design, or model cannot do.
5. `Possibility` states a candidate explanation or method before it is evaluated.
6. `Answer` gives a direct answer to a preceding question.
7. `Idea` gives the conceptual move behind a method.
8. `Approach` states the analysis that will be used.
9. `Goal` states the quantity or relation the method must recover.
10. `Data` identifies what was measured and how the observations are organized.
11. `Method` gives the inferential or computational procedure.
12. `Implementation` gives the ordered operational steps.
13. `Output` states what the method returns.
14. `Result #1`, `Result #2`, and so forth enumerate distinct empirical results.
15. `Interpretation` states what a result licenses.
16. `Important Point #1`, `Important Point #2`, and so forth separate consequences that do different work.
17. `Upshot` states the local conclusion.
18. `Interim Discussion` closes a major part and prepares the next question.
19. `Conclusion` marks the final synthesis in a research presentation. A course module normally ends with `Module Summary`.

Do not invent synonyms merely to vary the labels. In particular, do not replace `Question` with `Prompt`, `Challenge` with `Pain point`, `Idea` with `Insight`, or `Upshot` with `Key takeaway`. When a role label is unnecessary, use a descriptive title rather than another generic label.

When a role label is used, it is not followed by punctuation. It sits on its own line in bold. The explanatory text begins on the next line.

## Wording choices

Visible prose is compact and declarative. The default pattern in a course module is a descriptive title followed by one complete statement, example, or question. Two sentences are used when the second sharpens or restricts the first.

Questions normally begin with `What`, `Why`, or `How`.

1. `What kinds of things do we know when we know ...?`
2. `Where does evidence for ... come from?`
3. `How do we know what the relationship between ... should look like?`
4. `Why would we see ... if ...?`

Answers begin directly with the analysis. They do not repeat the question.

1. `Aggregate measures derived from ...`
2. `Treat the unobserved values as ...`
3. `Compare predictions for ...`

Challenges name the source of the difficulty.

1. `The response is bounded, but the model assigns positive probability outside the bounds.`
2. `The observations repeat participants and items, so the errors are not independent.`

Upshots state only the consequence established by the preceding evidence. They do not inflate its importance.

The prose often uses `the fact that`, `a particular`, `in terms of`, `with respect to`, `for instance`, `that is`, `thus`, and sentence-initial `But` or `This`. These forms should appear when they carry the argument. They should not be inserted as decoration.

Do not use generic presentation language such as `Today we will explore`, `Let us dive into`, `Key takeaway`, `Why this matters`, `The big picture`, or `Putting it all together`.

Do not use em dashes or en dashes. Do not use dash characters as separators in enumerations. Use sentences, numbered lists, commas, or parentheses.

## Text density and pacing

The Penn, Oxford, and Mayfest decks have a median of 13 visible words per slide. Their 75th percentiles are 24, 24, and 32 words. The NALOMA deck is denser, with a median of 25 words and a 75th percentile of 38 words.

Use the following limits.

1. A standard explanatory slide should normally contain 12 to 35 visible words.
2. A roadmap or method slide may contain up to 55 visible words when the items are distinct and load bearing.
3. A slide with a plot, linguistic example, or equation should use less prose because the visual already carries part of the explanation.
4. A slide should not exceed 70 visible words unless it reproduces source material, a questionnaire item, or a code example that must be read as a unit.
5. When content exceeds these limits, split it into a build sequence. Do not shrink the type.

The long academic decks repeatedly use adjacent slides that differ only in a new annotation, comparison, or highlighted region. The Penn deck contains at least 36 such adjacent builds, the Oxford deck at least 31, and the Mayfest deck at least 16. The course decks must preserve this pacing. Reveal one inferential step at a time.

## Slide-level compositions

The references use a limited set of compositions.

### Title slide

The title slide uses a dark navy background. The topical title is large, white, left aligned, and limited to two or three lines. Author and venue information is small and placed below the title. A yellow line illustration occupies the lower right. A QR code appears only when it points to a useful course or data resource.

### Section divider

The section divider uses the same dark navy background and yellow line illustration. The section title is large, white, and left aligned near the vertical center. A short white subtitle may appear below it. Nothing else appears on the slide.

### Single proposition

A single sentence is centered vertically within a wide text area. This form is used for a claim that should land before evidence is added. The CHiVe deck uses this form repeatedly with 48 pixel text.

### Descriptive title and statement

A descriptive title appears at the upper left. One statement, example, equation, or question follows below it. The left edge is shared by both. This is the default explanatory slide in a course module.

### Role and statement

A bold role label appears at the upper left. One statement follows below it. Use this composition when the rhetorical function of the slide is itself useful to the student. It is not the default composition.

### Two aligned blocks

Two labeled blocks are stacked vertically when the relation between them can be read on one slide. The labels should name the content whenever possible, such as `Observed count` and `Relative frequency`. Rhetorical pairs such as `Question` and `Answer` may be used when the relation itself organizes the discussion.

### Example with annotation

The linguistic example or graphical object is centered. Explanatory labels are placed directly beside the relevant span, point, arrow, or region. A separate legend is avoided when direct labeling is possible.

### Paper or dataset

A paper first page, dataset interface, or corpus excerpt occupies most of the slide. A short role label or one-sentence claim appears beside it. The citation is visible in small type at the lower right. The object is not placed inside a decorative card.

### Plot sequence

The plot initially appears with only the axes and observations required for the question. Later slides add a line, interval, annotation, comparison, or highlighted subset. Axes are labeled directly. Grid lines are light. Legends are used only when direct labels would be unclear.

### Code or model sequence

Only the code required for the current step is visible. The next slide highlights or adds one expression. Code is not reduced to illegible screenshots. A prose slide states the question before the code sequence and an `Upshot` slide follows it.

## Typography

The academic decks use Helvetica for almost all content. Verdana appears on some section dividers. The newest short demonstration uses Geist, but this is specific to that product identity. Course decks should use Helvetica with Arial as the fallback.

At 1280 by 720 pixels, the recurring rendered sizes are as follows.

1. Deck title: 64 pixels
2. Section title: 53.33 pixels
3. Role label: 42.67 pixels
4. Standard body text: 37.33 pixels
5. Dense roadmap or method text: 32 pixels
6. Title metadata: 26.67 pixels
7. Code and secondary annotation: 21.33 to 26.67 pixels
8. Source citation: 13.33 to 16 pixels

Role labels and titles are bold. Body text is regular. Italics are reserved for linguistic examples, cited work titles, variables in prose, and a contrast that genuinely depends on the distinction.

## Geometry

Slides use a 16 by 9 canvas at 1280 by 720 pixels.

1. The normal left and right margins are 88 to 96 pixels.
2. A wide centered proposition occupies roughly 1088 pixels.
3. Text blocks are aligned to a shared left edge.
4. Content begins near the upper third unless the slide contains one proposition, in which case it is vertically centered.
5. White space separates argumentative steps. It is not filled with decorative panels.
6. A slide may use two columns when a direct comparison requires them. It should not use a grid of cards.

## Color

The recurring section color is navy `#113A6A`. The line illustration is yellow `#FED105`. Content slides use white `#FFFFFF` with near-black text.

Color is semantic rather than decorative.

1. Orange marks a challenge, a highlighted alternative, or one side of a contrast.
2. Blue marks a second alternative, a structural relation, or a comparison series.
3. Green marks a goal, data source, or successful condition.
4. Red marks a contradiction, error, excluded region, or the particular element being inspected.

Do not assign every role label a different color. In the Penn, Oxford, and Mayfest decks, most role labels are black. Color is added only when it helps the current comparison.

## Equations and notation

An equation slide introduces one expression or one transformation. Notation is defined beside the expression or on the immediately preceding slide. A multi-line derivation is split so that each slide adds one line or highlights one substitution.

Do not introduce a concept through notation alone. Begin with the linguistic quantity, give the ordinary-language question, and then define the notation.

Use $\langle\cdot\rangle$ when defining a formally assembled structure, including a probability space, measurable space, observation key, structured outcome, or model specification. Use $(\cdot)$ for coordinate points, parameter vectors, intervals, and ordinary realized value tuples.

## Linguistic examples

Linguistic examples are complete enough to support the inference being discussed. Critical spans are colored or boxed. Minimally different examples are aligned so the changed material occupies the same position. Judgment symbols are explained before they are used.

Examples are not decorative. Each example should either motivate the concept, distinguish two analyses, support a computation, or test an interpretation.

## Citations and sources

A paper, dataset, nontrivial empirical claim, or externally sourced visual receives a visible short citation at the lower right. The complete URL or bibliographic information is also placed in the speaker notes using a `Sources` block.

Course textbook links should be preferred for statistical definitions. Dataset and linguistic claims should cite the relevant linguistic source.

## Quarto implementation rules

1. Use the shared Reveal theme in `decks/shared/theme.css`.
2. Use 1280 by 720 output with no transition animation.
3. Use `.section-slide` for dark dividers.
4. Use `.statement-slide` for a single centered proposition.
5. Use `.role-slide` only when an explicit rhetorical label helps the student follow the argument.
6. Use `.source` for the visible lower-right citation.
7. Use Reveal fragments only when each fragment adds one inferential step. Do not make every bullet incremental.
8. Preserve the same spatial anchors across a build sequence.
9. Put a `Sources` block in speaker notes for every externally sourced claim, dataset, plot, paper image, and visual.
10. Link every technical term at its first introduction to the same external authority used in the notes.
11. Mark every class meeting inside a multi-day module with a dated section divider.
12. Use content-bearing slide titles by default. A student should be able to identify the concept or operation from the title alone.

## Course-specific constraints

Each deck must follow the dependency order of the notes. A deck may remind students of an earlier term, but the reminder must link to the earlier notes page. It must not redefine the term as if it were new.

Each worked analysis must use the dataset assigned to that notes section. Do not import a later technique to make the example work. Do not reuse a dataset merely for convenience when the notes use a different dataset for the current method.

The slides must use standard statistical terminology. In particular, use `ordinary linear regression`, not `Gaussian regression`; use `out-of-sample prediction`, not `prediction target`; and do not coin local names for model failures or validation settings.
