Group heterogeneity and marginal dispersion

The Poisson dispersion page treated excess conditional variation as a diagnosis rather than a cause. The Poisson mixed-model page supplies one possible cause: persistent rate differences across known groups.

A Poisson regression without random effects can appear overdispersed when it omits a known grouping structure. Suppose speakers differ persistently in their filled pause rates. Pooling their segments under one Poisson mean mixes low-rate and high-rate speakers, so the combined count distribution varies more than a single Poisson distribution.

This extra variation arises by marginalizing over heterogeneous groups. It is not another response distribution. It is the difference between variation conditional on a random effect and variation after that effect has been averaged over.

Begin with the conditional Poisson claim

For segment i from speaker j, suppose

Y_{ij}\mid b_j \sim \operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda_{ij}(b_j)),

with

\log\lambda_{ij}(b_j) = \log t_{ij}+\eta_{ij}+b_j

and

b_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2).

The quantity \eta_{ij} contains the fixed intercept and predictors. Conditional on a particular value of b_j,

\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}\mid b_j) = \operatorname{E}(Y_{ij}\mid b_j) = \lambda_{ij}(b_j).

Thus the model is still Poisson within the conditional statement. The speaker effect does not insert a free residual variance into that Poisson distribution.

Average over the speaker population

Now consider a segment from a new speaker whose random effect has not been observed. Its expected count averages over the random effect distribution:

m_{ij} = \operatorname{E}_{b} [\lambda_{ij}(b)].

The law of total variance separates two sources of variation:

\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}) = \operatorname{E}_{b} [\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}\mid b)] + \operatorname{Var}_{b} [\operatorname{E}(Y_{ij}\mid b)].

Substitute the conditional Poisson mean and variance:

\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}) = \operatorname{E}_{b}[\lambda_{ij}(b)] + \operatorname{Var}_{b}[\lambda_{ij}(b)].

The first term is the marginal mean m_{ij}. The second term records variation in expected counts across speakers. Thus

\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}) = m_{ij} + \operatorname{Var}_{b}[\lambda_{ij}(b)].

If \tau>0, speaker baselines vary, so the second term is positive. The marginal variance then exceeds the marginal mean even though every conditional response distribution is Poisson.

Calculate the extra variation

The normal random intercept becomes log normal after exponentiation. For the model above,

m_{ij} = t_{ij}\exp(\eta_{ij}) \exp\left(\frac{\tau^2}{2}\right).

The variance of the conditional expected count is

\operatorname{Var}_{b}[\lambda_{ij}(b)] = m_{ij}^2 [\exp(\tau^2)-1].

The marginal count variance is thus

\operatorname{Var}(Y_{ij}) = m_{ij} + m_{ij}^2[\exp(\tau^2)-1].

Suppose the marginal expected count is 3 and \tau=.6. Then

3^2[\exp(.6^2)-1] \approx 3.90.

The marginal variance is about

3+3.90=6.90.

The extra 3.90 is not unexplained observation noise. Under this model, it arises because new speakers have different expected rates.

See why repeated rows are associated

Two segments from the same speaker share b_j. If one segment indicates that the speaker has a high baseline rate, another segment from that speaker also becomes more likely to have a high count. Averaging over the unknown speaker effect thus induces positive association among the speaker’s rows.

Segments from different speakers do not share this deviation because their random effects are independent under the model. The random intercept thus explains a patterned source of variation. It increases marginal dispersion and represents which rows should vary together.

This structure distinguishes a group-level random effect from an observation-level dispersion adjustment. A negative binomial response can represent count variation beyond a Poisson distribution. But an independent observation-level adjustment does not by itself say that several segments share a persistent speaker rate. The grouping term is licensed by known repetition in the design.

Diagnose at the intended level

A Pearson dispersion ratio from a Poisson regression without random effects can combine at least two sources: persistent group heterogeneity and remaining variation within groups. Adding the speaker random intercept may account for the first source. It need not account for the second.

After fitting the mixed model, ask whether the represented speaker variation matches the repeated data. If counts still vary too widely among comparable segments from the same speaker, the random intercept is not a complete account. The conditional mean may omit a predictor, the speaker effect may vary with a predictor, or the count response may need another variance relation.

The narrow conclusion is that a marginally overdispersed count distribution is compatible with a conditionally Poisson mixed model. It does not follow that every instance of overdispersion should be repaired with a random intercept. The design must identify a repeated unit, and the fitted group variation must account for the pattern actually observed.
