Bounded similarity judgments

White, Grimm, and Glass’s Experiment 2 asks whether two event descriptions that instantiate the same PropBank sense are judged more similar than descriptions that instantiate different senses. Participants rated within-verb pairs on an integer slider from 0, completely identical, to 100, extremely different. Lower values thus indicate greater perceived similarity.

We will first identify one contrast in the released data, then inspect the response support as on the bounded-response page, fit a linear mixed model as a benchmark, and replace that benchmark with ordered-beta regression. The random-intercept page introduced the repeated-participant and repeated-item structure used by both models.

NoteReading: White, Grimm, and Glass (2026), Experiment 2 through Analysis

White, Grimm, and Glass, Aspectual similarity predicts sense similarity.

Read the opening of Experiment 2 and its Procedure, Participants, Hypotheses, and Analysis sections.

Why this reading? These sections define the slider response, the repeated participant and sentence-pair design, and H2, the same-sense hypothesis adapted on this page. The paper represents same-sense similarity probabilistically; the teaching analysis replaces that score with binary equality of PropBank labels and retains only the conceptual contrast. The paper’s telicity and durativity predictors remain outside this application, so the reading also marks the boundary between the demonstrated claim and the full study.

Construct a within-verb teaching extract

The full study contains 10,800 test judgments. The preparation script constructs the teaching extract from that release. We retain test trials whose two descriptions are contentful, then restrict the data to verbs with both same-sense and different-sense pairs. This restriction ensures that the same-sense contrast is represented within every retained verb.

ratings <- read.csv(
  "../data/aspectual-similarity/within-verb-similarity.csv"
)

c(
  ratings = nrow(ratings),
  participants = length(unique(ratings$participant_id)),
  verbs = length(unique(ratings$verb)),
  pairs = length(unique(ratings$sentence_pair_id))
)

The result contains 4,405 ratings from 202 participants, 14 verbs, and 218 sentence pairs. One row is a participant by pair judgment. Participants and sentence pairs repeat, so neither the ratings nor the pair-level predictor are independently replicated 4,405 times.

The deterministic preparation script asserts these counts and verifies that every retained verb has both predictor values. The adapted CSV has SHA-256 checksum b19edbb7463a6767274e3dde78e902b0e8777424a21d7fdf231ea1e6d0e25f35.

Inspect the response before fitting

hist(
  ratings$dissimilarity,
  breaks = seq(-.5, 100.5, by = 1),
  xlab = "Dissimilarity rating",
  main = "Within-verb similarity judgments"
)

mean(ratings$dissimilarity %in% c(0, 100))
aggregate(dissimilarity ~ same_sense, ratings, mean)

Every integer value from 0 through 100 occurs. Exact endpoints account for 20.8% of the teaching extract, so standard beta regression cannot use the observed responses without altering them. Nudging one fifth of the observations inward would also make the arbitrary nudge part of one fifth of the pointwise likelihood contributions. Endpoint use varies across participants: some never use an endpoint, while others use one on every retained trial.

Same-sense pairs have a mean dissimilarity of 55.9; different-sense pairs have a mean of 76.4. This 20.4-point raw difference is theoretically relevant, but it does not yet account for repeated participants or pairs.

A linear mixed model benchmark

Fit a linear mixed-effects benchmark with normally distributed errors and participant and sentence-pair intercepts:

linear_fit <- lme4::lmer(
  dissimilarity ~ factor(same_sense) + verb_type +
    (1 | participant_id) + (1 | sentence_pair_id),
  data = ratings,
  REML = FALSE
)

The fit estimates a -20.1-point same-sense contrast. Its participant, sentence-pair, and residual standard deviations are 13.5, 14.7, and 21.3 points, respectively. Thus the contrast survives a basic dependence repair.

But the likelihood fails the support check. For fixed-effect predictions \widehat\mu_i and residual scale \widehat\sigma, compute

mu <- predict(linear_fit, re.form = NA)
sigma <- sigma(linear_fit)

illegal_mass <- mean(
  pnorm(0, mu, sigma) + 1 - pnorm(100, mu, sigma)
)

The result is 10.1%. The benchmark places about one tenth of its fixed-effect predictive mass outside a scale that participants could not leave. The coefficient answers the intended contrast, while the response distribution makes predictions the task cannot produce.

This is why model criticism should identify the specific discrepancy. “The residuals are nonnormal” would not tell us whether the problem is skew, tail weight, dependence, or support. Here the model assigns positive probability to values outside the response support, and that discrepancy is exact and measurable.

Replace the response distribution

Use the same predictor and dependence structure with an ordered-beta likelihood:

ratings$dissimilarity_01 <- ratings$dissimilarity / 100

ordered_beta_fit <- ordbetareg::ordbetareg(
  dissimilarity_01 ~ factor(same_sense) + verb_type +
    (1 | participant_id) + (1 | sentence_pair_id),
  data = ratings,
  chains = 4,
  cores = 4,
  warmup = 1000,
  iter = 3000,
  control = list(adapt_delta = .99),
  seed = 20260825
)

The audited teaching pilot estimates a same-sense coefficient of -.870 on the latent scale, with a 95% interval of [-1.052, -.692]. This coefficient is not measured in slider points.

We next check the endpoint predictions. The median predicted endpoint mass is 20.78%, with a 95% interval of [19.35%, 22.33%], compared with 20.82% in the observed data. The released full Experiment 2 model also recovers the zero mass, one mass, mean, and standard deviation in its complete data. Its reported convergence diagnostics do not show a computational warning.

The latent coefficient is not an 87-point response change. Its negative sign shifts the entire predictive distribution toward greater similarity: more probability near zero, lower interior values, and less probability at 100. The interpretation thus comes from predicted response distributions, not from multiplying the coefficient by 100.

Read the comparison in stages

The raw means show the expected direction: same-sense pairs receive lower dissimilarity ratings. The linear mixed model shows that the direction remains after accounting for repeated participants and sentence pairs. Its predictive distribution then reveals a separate problem, since about one tenth of its probability lies outside the response scale. Ordered-beta regression removes that support failure and reproduces the observed endpoint proportion.

The restricted extract answers a narrower question than the full paper. Exact PropBank label equality is directly observed here, while the paper’s full analysis propagates probabilistic sense, telicity, and durativity measures and permits richer by-verb variation. The teaching extract supports a comparison of same-sense and different-sense contentful pairs among verbs represented by both pair types. It does not show that one 20-point contrast applies to every English verb.

LING 414 extension

Return to the full released analysis and replace exact same-sense with its probabilistic score. Then compare a fixed-effects-only fit with the dependence-aware model for the telicity-by-same-sense and durativity-by-same-sense interactions. The telicity interaction disappears after participant and sentence-pair structure enters, while the durativity interaction shows the reverse pattern. This contrast supplies a concrete pseudoreplication diagnosis, but it should follow the simpler bounded-response lesson rather than serve as its entry point.

Data, license, and citation

The source paper is White, Grimm, and Glass (2026), Aspectual similarity predicts sense similarity. The companion repository records the analysis code, current paper source, and cleaned data. The data are licensed CC BY-SA 4.0; the teaching extract is shared under the same terms. Ordered-beta regression follows Kubinec.

NoteTwo recurring questions

What counts as a new observation? For the narrow fitted model, a new judgment may come from an observed participant and pair; a stronger claim requires new participants, new pairs, or new verbs and a validation design that holds out the corresponding unit.

What breaks the simpler model? Exact endpoints and 10.1% predictive probability outside the permitted response range break the response model even though the mean same-sense contrast is stable.
