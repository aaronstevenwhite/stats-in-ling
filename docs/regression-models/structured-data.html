Repeated units in linguistic data

The chapter on independent sampling separated the number of recorded observations from the assumptions that make those observations independent. Linguistic experiments often contain repeated participants and items, so the rows inherit dependence from the design.

Consider an acceptability experiment with 60 participants and 40 sentence items. Every participant judges several items, and every item is judged by several participants. The trial table may have 2,400 rows, but those rows are not 2,400 unrelated pieces of information.

A row records one response. It does not necessarily identify the unit that was independently sampled or the unit across which a claim should generalize.

NoteReading: Winter (2020), Sections 14.2 and 14.3

Winter, The independence assumption and Dealing with non independence via experimental design and averaging, in Statistics for Linguists.

Why this reading? These sections begin with repeated linguistic observations and show how design and aggregation affect dependence. They are included here because students need to identify the repeated units before a mixed model formula is introduced.

See repetition in the table

participant item condition rating
p01 s01 control 6
p01 s02 target 5
p02 s01 control 7
p02 s02 target 4

Rows 1 and 2 share participant p01. Rows 1 and 3 share item s01. These links come from the design rather than from the numerical values of the ratings.

Responses from p01 may resemble one another because that participant uses the scale in a persistent way. Responses to s01 may resemble one another because that sentence has a persistent level of acceptability. Both sources can remain after the condition predictor has been included.

Thus the table contains at least three kinds of units:

  1. 2,400 response rows;
  2. 60 sampled participants;
  3. 40 sampled sentence items.

The counts answer different questions. The first tells us how many judgments were recorded. The second and third tell us how broadly the condition comparison was replicated across people and linguistic materials.

Write the independence claim

A regression model without grouping terms often represents conditional independence with

p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol\beta) =\prod_i p(y_i\mid\mathbf{x}_i,\boldsymbol\beta).

After the predictors are known, the joint probability is written as a product over rows. Under this model, learning the residual for one row gives no information about the residual for another.

Dependence arises when two rows share an unrepresented cause. If p01 tends to use higher ratings, observing one high response from p01 changes what we should expect for another response from p01. If s01 is persistently difficult, observing one low rating for s01 changes what we should expect from another participant on s01.

Treating these rows as unrelated can make uncertainty too small. It can also allow a few unusual participants or items to exert more influence than their number of independent units warrants.

Distinguish crossed and nested units

Participant and item are crossed when participants encounter many items and items are encountered by many participants. The four row table above has this structure.

A nested structure has a different arrangement. Suppose a corpus contains utterances from interviews, and each interview records exactly one speaker. Utterances are nested within interviews, and interviews are nested within speakers. An interview cannot belong to two speakers under that design.

Nested grouping describes containment among observational units. It differs from one statistical model being nested within another, which means that a parameter restriction turns the larger model into the smaller model.

The distinction can be checked by asking two questions:

  1. Can one level of unit A occur with several levels of unit B?
  2. Can one level of unit B occur with several levels of unit A?

Two yes answers indicate crossing. If each level of B belongs to only one level of A, B is nested within A. Column order and identifier spelling do not determine this relation.

Match dependence to the claim

A participant population claim requires replication across participants. An item population claim requires replication across items. A claim about both requires a design and analysis that preserve both sources.

For instance, a condition difference observed across 60 participants but built from only two target sentences has extensive participant replication and weak item replication. Adding more judgments of the same two sentences does not create new sentence evidence. The distinction among response, sampling, and generalization units thus concerns interpretation as well as standard errors.

See why averaging is partial

Averaging all trials within each participant produces 60 rows. This representation preserves participant replication, but it removes item variation and any trial predictor that changes within the average. Averaging within each item produces 40 rows and loses participant variation.

Aggregation may answer a deliberately chosen question, but it cannot preserve both crossed sources in one ordinary row level analysis. Mixed effects models retain the trial responses while representing the shared participant and item variation.

Before writing such a model, record the possible sources of dependence:

  1. State what one response row represents.
  2. List every sampled unit that appears on more than one row.
  3. State whether the units are crossed or nested.
  4. State the populations across which the linguistic comparison should generalize.

This list describes the design. It should be possible to write it before deciding on software or a response distribution.

NoteCheck the row count

In a study with 50 participants, 30 items, and one judgment per participant and item pair, how many trial rows are possible? How many sampled units exist at each grouping level? Which count measures item replication?
