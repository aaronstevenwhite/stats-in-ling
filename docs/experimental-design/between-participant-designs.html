Between-participant designs

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

Suppose an experiment tests whether two days of familiar accent training improves word recognition. Once a participant receives that training, the participant cannot return to the original untrained state. Asking the same person to complete an untrained condition afterward would compare two moments in a learning process, not trained and untrained versions of the same initial listener.

In a between-participant design, each participant contributes observations to only one condition. The condition effect is estimated by comparing groups rather than by subtracting two responses from the same participant. The resulting independence structure is the one used in the earlier chapter on comparing two independent means. This design preserves the meaning of a condition that has a lasting effect, though it also exposes the comparison to participant variation across groups.

The comparison is across groups

Let Y_i be one participant level response, such as mean recognition accuracy after training, and let Z_i indicate the assigned condition. If Z_i=1 denotes familiar accent training and Z_i=0 denotes unfamiliar accent training, the estimated group difference is

\widehat\Delta=\bar Y_1-\bar Y_0,

where

\bar Y_z=\frac{1}{n_z}\sum_{i:Z_i=z}Y_i.

Each participant appears on only one side of this subtraction. Stable participant characteristics thus do not cancel within a difference score. Under the random assignment mechanism, preassignment characteristics and condition are independent, but realized groups can still differ by chance.

Work through a small comparison

Suppose four participants receive familiar accent training and four receive unfamiliar accent training. Their recognition proportions are:

participant training proportion correct
P1 familiar .82
P2 familiar .74
P3 familiar .79
P4 familiar .85
P5 unfamiliar .70
P6 unfamiliar .68
P7 unfamiliar .76
P8 unfamiliar .72

The familiar group mean is

\bar Y_1=\frac{.82+.74+.79+.85}{4}=.80,

and the unfamiliar group mean is

\bar Y_0=\frac{.70+.68+.76+.72}{4}=.715.

Thus, the estimated difference is .80-.715=.085, or 8.5 percentage points. The estimate describes the difference between the two realized groups. Its uncertainty must include the fact that different participants appear in the two conditions.

Code 
recognition <- data.frame(
  participant = paste0("P", 1:8),
  training = rep(c("familiar", "unfamiliar"), each = 4),
  proportion_correct = c(.82, .74, .79, .85, .70, .68, .76, .72)
)

group_means <- aggregate(
  proportion_correct ~ training,
  data = recognition,
  FUN = mean
)

group_means
with(recognition, t.test(
  proportion_correct[training == "familiar"],
  proportion_correct[training == "unfamiliar"]
))

The two vectors passed to t.test() contain different participants, so the call does not use paired = TRUE. Treating the values as paired would invent a relation between P1 and P5, P2 and P6, and so on that the design never created.

Trials improve measurement, not assignment replication

Each participant may complete many recognition trials. Those trials can make that participant’s recognition proportion less sensitive to one unusually easy or difficult word. They do not replace additional independently assigned participants when the target is a population difference between training conditions.

Suppose P1 completes 400 trials while every other participant completes 40. The study still contains eight independently assigned participants. A trial weighted mean would allow P1 to contribute ten times as much as another participant, which changes the target toward a randomly selected recorded trial. A participant weighted mean gives each assigned participant one contribution, which matches a target over participants.

Items can vary within participants even when condition varies only between participants. The design record should thus state both that training is assigned between participants and that word responses repeat within participants and items. The earlier mixed effects model sequence uses that record to represent the shared variation.

When this design is needed

Training, prolonged adaptation, and explicit instruction may have lasting effects. A between-participant design is appropriate when exposure to one condition changes how the participant would respond in another. It can also reduce demand effects when seeing multiple conditions would reveal the intended contrast.

The cost is that participant variation contributes directly to uncertainty. If a strong participant characteristic such as baseline proficiency is measured before assignment, the restricted randomization procedure introduced on the previous page can balance condition counts within proficiency groups. The same baseline measure should be retained in the analysis plan. This use is legitimate because the restriction is set before outcomes are observed.

Do not invent pairs

A common failure is to arrange the two group vectors in some convenient order and run a paired analysis. The row positions then masquerade as pairs even though one participant has no natural counterpart in the other condition. The paired analysis usually understates uncertainty because it removes variation through an association that the design did not provide.

Another failure is to count trials as though condition were independently assigned to every trial. Both failures can be detected by writing the unit of assignment before writing the analysis.

  4. Under what condition may a baseline proficiency measure be used to restrict assignment?

Summing up

A between-participant design compares groups whose members received only one condition. It protects manipulations that cannot be reset, while participant differences remain part of the group comparison. Within either participant structure, sentence versions must still be distributed so that item identity is not confused with condition. That is the role of counterbalancing.
