Posterior distributions

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code
  • View Source

The foundations chapter derived Bayes’ rule, and the frequentist sequence distinguished a likelihood from a probability distribution over observations. We now use both distinctions to construct a posterior distribution. Suppose a listener hears an ambiguous reciprocal sentence and chooses either a collective interpretation or a distributive interpretation.

Bayesian inference represents uncertainty about \Theta with a probability distribution. The distribution before observing the present responses is the prior distribution. The distribution after observing them is the posterior distribution.

Begin with a finite parameter space

To make the update visible by hand, suppose the model initially permits three possible response probabilities:

\Theta\in\{.25,.50,.75\}.

Assign prior probabilities

\theta p(\theta)
.25 .20
.50 .50
.75 .30

These numbers describe uncertainty about the parameter before the present sample. They do not say that a response is 20%, 50%, or 30% likely to be collective. Conditional on a particular \theta, the Bernoulli model supplies that response probability.

Now observe three collective responses and one distributive response. For a fixed \theta, the likelihood kernel is

p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta) \propto \theta^3(1-\theta).

For fixed \theta, p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta) is the sampling distribution over possible data. Once \mathbf{x} is observed, the same expression viewed as a function of \theta is the likelihood. It is not a probability distribution over the three parameter values.

Multiply prior support by data support

Bayes’ rule is

p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x}) = \frac{ p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)p(\theta) }{ p(\mathbf{x}) }.

For the finite parameter space, we can update in three steps. First, evaluate the likelihood at each value. Second, multiply by the corresponding prior probability. Third, divide each product by the sum of all three products.

\theta likelihood kernel prior unnormalized posterior
.25 .01171875 .20 .00234375
.50 .0625 .50 .03125
.75 .10546875 .30 .031640625

The normalizing constant is

Z =.00234375+.03125+.031640625 =.065234375.

Dividing each product by Z gives posterior probabilities of approximately .0359, .4791, and .4850.

Code 
theta <- c(.25, .50, .75)
prior <- c(.20, .50, .30)
likelihood_kernel <- theta^3 * (1 - theta)
unnormalized <- prior * likelihood_kernel
posterior <- unnormalized / sum(unnormalized)

stopifnot(abs(sum(posterior) - 1) < 1e-12)
stopifnot(isTRUE(all.equal(
  posterior,
  c(0.03592814, 0.47904192, 0.48502994),
  tolerance = 1e-7
)))

data.frame(theta, prior, likelihood_kernel, posterior)

The data favor larger values because three of four responses were collective. The prior gives substantial mass to .50, so the posterior probabilities for .50 and .75 are nearly equal. This result is an update from a particular prior through a particular likelihood.

Understand the normalizer

The denominator

p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{\theta} p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)p(\theta)

is the prior predictive probability of the observed data, up to any likelihood factor that is common to all parameter values. It does not depend on which \theta is being evaluated. Dividing by it makes the posterior probabilities sum to one.

For a continuous parameter, the sum becomes an integral:

p(\mathbf{x}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)p(\theta) \,\mathrm{d}\theta.

We often write

p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x}) \propto p(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)p(\theta)

when we first care about posterior shape. A normalized density is still required before assigning posterior probabilities or calculating expectations.

Interpret a continuous density

For a continuous parameter, p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x}) is a density rather than the probability that \Theta equals exactly \theta. The probability of any single point is zero. Posterior probability is assigned to an interval by integrating:

\mathbb{P}(a<\Theta<b\mid\mathbf{x}) = \int_a^b p(\theta\mid\mathbf{x}) \,\mathrm{d}\theta.

A higher density at .75 than at .50 says that a small neighborhood around .75 receives more posterior mass per unit width. It does not assign positive probability to the exact point .75 in a continuous model.

Keep the conditioning direction visible

The error of transposing a conditional probability also applies here. The likelihood evaluates the observed data as a function of a candidate parameter. The posterior distributes uncertainty over parameter values after conditioning on those data. Bayes’ rule connects the two quantities, but it does not make them interchangeable.

Posterior uncertainty is also not computational error. In the hand calculation, the posterior probabilities are exact under the model. Later simulation methods will approximate posterior summaries and add Monte Carlo error, but that error is not part of the posterior itself.
