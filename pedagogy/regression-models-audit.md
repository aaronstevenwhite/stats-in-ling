# Audit of the regression-model notes

## Scope and standard

This report audits all 64 `.qmd` pages directly under `regression-models/`. It does not audit or rewrite the archived slides. Each page was read against the same explanatory chain:

1. motivate a linguistic problem;
2. identify the data source, observational unit, example observation, measures, and any preprocessing or aggregation;
3. link to the first full treatment of each prerequisite and give a short in-place reminder;
4. justify the response distribution, link, parameterization, algorithm, and tuning choices;
5. derive the result without algebraic jumps, while distinguishing random variables, observed values, estimators, and estimates;
6. bridge the displayed mathematics to the code and then to the printed output or plot;
7. interpret the result on a scientifically meaningful scale and state its limits; and
8. test likely misconceptions, summarize the page, and point forward.

Severity codes are **H** (the current page breaks the explanatory chain in several places or introduces a major object without enough motivation or derivation), **M** (the chain is present but one or two substantial links are missing), and **L** (the page is already comparatively strong and needs local additions).

## Cross-cutting diagnosis

### 1. The notes often begin one abstraction too late

The strongest pages begin with a scientific comparison and only then name the statistical object. The weaker pages begin with a finished definition: “A generalized linear model has three distinct parts,” “Many GLM response distributions share the exponential-family form,” or “Quasi-Poisson regression keeps the Poisson mean function.” These claims are correct, but they omit the question-repair sequence that characterizes the reference pedagogy: pose the linguistic question, try the tempting model, show the observable failure, name the repair, and only then formalize it.

Every major sequence should thus use a named problem. The Provo sequence needs a **reader-to-position compression problem**; the binary sequence needs a **support-and-variance problem**; the count sequence needs an **exposure-and-dispersion problem**; and the mixed-model sequence needs a **row-versus-unit problem**. These names should be introduced once and reused.

### 2. Dataset introductions identify rows but rarely make a row imaginable

The notes often say what one row represents, but they seldom show an actual row and read its columns aloud. The Provo pages never define a first fixation in student-facing prose, never show a source passage fragment or explain why passage text is absent from the teaching table, and do not explain why the table averages log duration across readers beyond saying that reader effects are lost. The MALD item pages do not walk through a word such as *abandon* and distinguish acoustic duration, response time, SUBTLEX frequency, phone count, syllable count, and the number of contributing correct trials. Similar problems recur for SuperTelicity, MegaVeridicality, Nettle, Storme, MegaNegRaising, and UCCA.

Each dataset thus needs one canonical “data card” containing: (i) the elicitation or corpus event; (ii) one source-cited or explicitly marked synthetic stimulus when source text cannot be redistributed; (iii) one actual row; (iv) a field-by-field reading; (v) inclusion and exclusion rules; (vi) the unit at which rows are independent or repeated; and (vii) a reason for the chosen aggregation. Later pages should link to the card and restate the one fact that matters locally.

### 3. Backlinks are uneven

Twenty-two of the 64 pages contain no link to another `.qmd` page. This is concentrated in the generalized-model sequence, where students encounter the exponential family, sufficiency, IRLS, deviance, odds, logistic regression, quasi-likelihood, negative-binomial regression, and ordered-beta regression with few reminders of the probability and inference machinery on which those pages depend.

A backlink should not replace explanation. The useful pattern is: “Recall from [maximum likelihood] that the observations are held fixed while the parameter varies. Here that means ….” This supplies both the dependency and the local reading.

### 4. Formula-to-formula transitions are more common than derivations

Several pages present a result after “differentiating,” “because,” or “the posterior is again” without doing the intermediate work. The largest gaps occur in the OLS normal equations, the covariance of the OLS estimator, the Student-t basis for conventional intervals, the normal–inverse-gamma posterior, the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell result, IRLS, deviance, quasi-Poisson uncertainty, and the ordered-beta likelihood. The notation also sometimes moves from random variables to observed values without announcing the move.

The reference pedagogy treats notation as a contract: write the random object, define the observed realization, define shorthand, read the shorthand in words, and recall the full form when the inferential interpretation depends on it. These pages should adopt that contract explicitly.

### 5. Model and algorithm choices are frequently stated rather than defended

The notes often justify support but not the full distributional choice. A binary support motivates Bernoulli, for instance, but does not by itself motivate the logit rather than probit or complementary log-log link. Nonnegative integer support motivates a count family, but not Poisson equidispersion. Endpoint-inclusive sliders motivate a mixed discrete–continuous family, but not the shared ordered location assumed by ordered beta. The code also introduces `BFGS`, starting values, ten folds, a penalty grid, the one-standard-error rule, `bobyqa`, `maxfun`, and `nAGQ = 1` with little or no explanation.

Every such choice needs three sentences: what restriction it adds, what feature of the design or computation motivates it, and what observable pattern would count against it. For tuning constants, distinguish a mathematical requirement, a computational default, and a pedagogical convenience.

### 6. The linear sequence has a student-facing close; most later sequences do not

Twenty-five pages have a “Check your understanding” section and 36 have an explicit forward link. Nearly all of the short GLM pages and all of the long mixed-model pages lack misconception-oriented checks. Long pages do state limitations, but students are not asked to distinguish the objects most likely to be confused: probability versus odds, conditional versus marginal predictions, a residual versus an error, a group deviation versus a group-specific coefficient, overdispersion versus zero inflation, and a singular optimum versus optimizer failure.

Every page should close with three or four questions and hidden answers, followed by one short upshot and a forward dependency. At each module boundary, add a numbered recap of the named objects and two concrete “live possibilities” for the next module.

### 7. The prose is largely free of lexical AI slop, but it is too compressed to match the teaching voice

The lexical scan found none of the project’s banned expressions in the 64 pages, and the notes do not rely on decorative em dashes. The main style problem is structural, not lexical. Many paragraphs are compressed reference prose: definition, equation, code, limitation. They rarely use “Suppose,” “Consider,” “So,” “But,” or “That is” to walk through a learner’s likely reasoning; they rarely show a tempting wrong reading and repair it; and they often end on a caveat without landing the positive upshot. The revision should preserve the plain diction while adding named objects, short interpretive sentences, learner-directed questions, and explicit bridges.

## Prioritized implementation plan

1. **Build the nine data cards first.** Expand the canonical anchors for Provo, MALD items, MALD trials, SuperTelicity, MegaOrientation, MegaVeridicality, Nettle, Storme, MegaNegRaising, and UCCA before editing downstream pages. This work removes repetition while giving every later page a stable place to link.
2. **Repair the inferential derivation spine.** In order: OLS normal equations → OLS estimator distribution and Student-t intervals → normal–inverse-gamma posterior → GLM likelihood → IRLS → deviance/LRT → quasi-likelihood → negative-binomial mixture → ordered-beta likelihood → Laplace. Later pages should rely on these derivations but still give a two-sentence reminder.
3. **Add a standard “model choice” box to every model-family page.** The box should list support, mean, variance, link, independence/conditioning claim, why the choice is plausible here, and one observable failure mode.
4. **Add explicit math → code → output bridges.** Before each nontrivial chunk, name the mathematical object being computed. After it, point to the exact printed value or graphical feature that instantiates the preceding equation. Avoid chunks that silently perform data preparation, fitting, prediction, and validation at once.
5. **Complete the navigation graph.** Add prerequisite backlinks and in-place reminders to the 22 pages with no `.qmd` link, then add a forward link and “Check your understanding” section to every page. Use misconception pairs rather than recall-only questions.
6. **Revise in teaching-voice passes, not page order.** First add named problems and motivating questions; second add examples and derivations; third shorten paragraphs into orientation/formalization/interpretation units; fourth add “Suppose/Consider/So/But/That is” bridges and short upshots; fifth run the ASW style and slop audits.
7. **Render and test one complete branch before propagating the pattern.** Use the Provo linear branch as the reference implementation, then the SuperTelicity GLM branch, then the mixed-model branch. Verify links, execution, figures, hidden answers, and consistency of notation at each stage.
